The problem

Sharing a playlist means sharing an app, not a taste

People's listening is split across services. The playlist you made for a road trip is on Spotify; the one you actually fall asleep to is on YouTube. Sharing all of it means pasting three links and explaining which is which.

A link-in-bio tool can hold those links, but it flattens them into identical rows. A playlist is not a row. It has a cover, a mood, a length, and none of that survives being listed as blue text.

So the brief became narrow on purpose: one page, one link, and the playlists have to look like playlists.

The insight

The cover art is the interface

Every playlist already ships with the only visual anybody recognises. Lead with the artwork and the page explains itself before a word is read.

That reframed the page from a list into a shelf, something you flick through rather than scan. It also set the constraint the rest of the build answered to: if the artwork is the interface, the chrome has to get out of its way.

The front door

A homepage that plays the product instead of describing it

The first homepage was a headline, a halftone panel and one button. It said what SpindlShare was. The second one shows it running, and everything on it is real: the records on the changer, the links in the marquee and the songs on the turntable are the playlists on a real public shelf.

That's a rule, not a flourish. The page is built from that shelf and refreshed hourly, so it can never advertise a product that looks different from the one that ships. If the read fails it gets empty lists rather than an error, and still ships, just without the art.

The name you type comes with you

A name typed on the homepage is checked live, with the same rules and endpoint as onboarding, and rides along in the session: after Google, it's already in the field. A taken name holds you on the page; no name at all doesn't, since one can be picked after signing in.

The old front door became the way in

Every "Claim your link" leads to /signup, the original homepage, which keeps its own sign-in card. Nothing that worked was thrown away to make room for the new page.

Why one button. Every field on a sign-up form is a place to leave. The account exists only to own a username and a list, so there is nothing worth asking for that Google does not already answer.

The email path is built and switched off. It needs a Resend key, which this deployment has no budget for, so the live site offers Google alone. Its screens are built and the code behind them ships; it turns on with one environment variable.

The object

Modelled, not drawn

The changer started as CSS: flat discs seen from above, the grooves a repeating gradient and the shine a conic one laid over the top. It read as a diagram of a record rather than a record, and up close the gradient's rings shimmered into moiré. So it was rebuilt in three.js, in real units of a record's radius.

Vinyl shines the way it does because its grooves are concentric. Light streaks radially across the record, and the streak stays put while the record turns. That comes from an anisotropic material whose grain runs along the grooves, over a procedural groove map that leaves glossy gaps between the tracks.

  • A dark studio, not a bright room. The stock room environment washed the coloured pressings out to pastel. A handful of softboxes in the dark gives crisp highlights and keeps a ruby pressing ruby.
  • Labels printed from the cover. Each playlist's artwork is printed onto a paper label, with the wordmark set round the top, the playlist's name round the bottom and SIDE A down the side.
  • A deck worth looking at. Book-matched walnut, a brass inlay, a machined platter with strobe dots and a lamp to read them by, 33 lit, and a gimballed S-shaped arm with a brass counterweight.
  • Motion with weight. The changer drops each record under gravity; the platter catches it and takes the spin, and the stack settles by one. On the player, the platter spins up and the arm lowers only while a song is actually playing.
  • One model, three uses. The hero changer, the player turntable, and the records in the step sleeves, which are the same model rendered once into images instead of three more live scenes.
  • Paid for on arrival. three is split out of the first load: the hero's scene is fetched once the page has painted, the rest only as their section comes near, and with reduced motion every scene holds still.

The back cover

Sand on a plate

The page ends on a Chladni plate. Sand on a vibrating plate is thrown off the parts that move and collects on the lines that don't, and every three seconds a new frequency kicks the plate and the sand runs into a new figure.

The figure is the standing wave of a free square plate, cos(nπx)cos(mπy) − cos(mπx)cos(nπy), and the sand gathers where it's zero. Each grain drifts toward the nearest still line and jitters with the plate's hum, so even a settled figure never quite stops buzzing, and the pointer shakes grains loose like a finger on the plate.

The plate, running live

A new frequency every 3 seconds. Drag across it.

The shipped modes, wave and constants, drawn on a 2D canvas here instead of in three.js. The same rules pick the plate: narrower than 700px, it becomes the phone's 160px band.

On a phone, it fell apart. The band was 122px tall, split into three plates 130px wide, and the grain count followed the band's area, which gave a phone about 1,400 grains against 13,000 on a desktop. But sand only reads as lines, and a small plate has short ones: what matters is how many grains land on each. Counted by line length instead, plates times plate size, a phone gets 2,500, in a fixed 160px band holding one wide plate.

The craft

Small decisions that make a shelf feel like an object

Two layouts, one shelf

A stacked deck for people with a handful of playlists, an arc carousel for people with many. Same data, same chrome; the owner picks in settings and the public page swaps the component.

Geometry as ratios, not pixels

The deck is a 184px card stepping 40px on both axes in a 393px frame, stored as fractions of the measured stage, so it holds its proportions on any screen instead of needing a constant per breakpoint.

One gesture, one card

Any scroll past a small threshold plays a fixed animation to completion and ignores further input until it lands. A flick and a shove move the deck by exactly the same amount.

Endless without duplication

Position wraps modulo the playlist count, so the shelf runs forever in both directions without the list ever being copied. Both ends fade, so cards dissolve in and out instead of popping at the window edge.

Stacked, running live

Scroll it, drag it, tap a card.

Not a rebuild: this is the shipped Deck component, with the same playlists the live page serves. A card steps 40/184ths of itself on both axes, and position wraps modulo the count so the run never ends.

Arc, running live

Same input, a different shelf.

The shipped Arc component. Covers ride a circle of radius 2.1 times the stage height, and a mask dissolves the ends before they can reach the chrome.

The arc's ends don't get cut off. They blur as they go, and a mask on the stage dissolves them before they can touch the header.

Culling covers at the band's edge ended the ribbon on a hard line. Fading each one individually let a cover sit at 0.39 opacity with half of it over the header. The mask is what actually guarantees nothing paints there.

The constraint

The API I designed for wasn't available to me

The original plan was to connect Spotify and YouTube, import every playlist, and store the songs. I built that: OAuth, token refresh, encrypted at rest, sync reconciliation, the lot.

Then the wall: both services gate the scopes bulk import needs behind an approved-organisation review. A solo project doesn't clear it. Everything I'd built assumed a door that wouldn't open.

So the product pivoted to the one route that works without permission: paste a link. The public oEmbed endpoint returns a playlist's title and cover art to anyone who asks. That's ten fields, and not one of them is a track.

For a while that meant no songs at all. Then they came back another way. Spotify's own embed page ships a playlist's whole track list in a data blob that needs no account, and YouTube's playlist page lists its first hundred videos in its initial data, YouTube Music's tracks among them. Neither is an API and neither promises anything, so the parser searches for the list instead of walking a fixed path, and every failure returns an empty one: a change on their side costs the song list, never the page.

What it cost. Both pages stop at 100 songs, and nothing without a login goes further. Rather than pass the first hundred off as the lot, the real size is read from the playlist page's own metadata, so a long playlist says "279 tracks" and "Showing 100 of 279".

What it saved. No OAuth, no tokens, no approval queue. Adding a playlist went from a four-screen connect flow to pasting a URL, which is how people share playlists anyway.

The refresh control

Press it. It reports, then takes itself away.

Why the note floats

Toggle both. Watch where the first row lands.

Inline, the note pushes every playlist down and pulls them back up again. Fixed, it costs no layout at all: on the real page the first row stays at 294px and the page height at 1021px, before, during and after.

The player

Their audio, our player

Spotify's embedded player only streams to a visitor signed in to Spotify, so on a public page it mostly sat there doing nothing, and a cross-origin iframe can't be restyled to match anything. So the song rows became the controls.

A Spotify song plays its 30-second preview, which anyone can hear, through a plain audio element. YouTube has no previews, so its songs play whole through YouTube's own player, kept on the page at zero opacity behind the same gold bar and rows: a video window above a song list read as a different product. That is a deliberate trade against YouTube's terms, which want the player visible, and it's the first thing to break if they enforce them.

Each service is named by its own icon rather than a coloured dot, and the page ends on the profile's address and wordmark.

The process

Built by measuring, not by eyeballing

Motion is the hard part of a shelf, and screenshots lie about it. So every interaction got checked by driving a headless browser and reading the DOM back for real numbers, on a real device size.

  • The spinning icon. Having the class isn't proof. The check reads the running animation and samples its angle twice: 100 degrees, then minus 167.
  • The floating card. The requirement was that nothing moves. Verified by measuring the first playlist's position and the page height before, during and after: 294px and 1021px, unchanged.
  • The label's contrast. A first pass said 20:1 and was wrong, because it was sampling the white text, not the cover behind it. Corrected, the real figure was 1.59:1: illegible. That's what put the gradient behind the titles.
  • The arc that wouldn't stop. In the homepage's phone, one scroll over the arc spun it for eight seconds. Inside an iframe, the timestamp the browser hands each animation frame ran eight seconds behind performance.now(), and each step started on one clock and was measured on the other. Everything that moves now times itself from the frame clock alone.
  • The wordmark's margin. A 2% gap either side of the giant wordmark, measured from the letters' ink rather than their box: at 14.4vw they filled 98% of the width, at 14.1vw they fill 96%. Padding the box instead pinned the word left, because a centred line wider than its box falls back to the start edge.
  • The avatars that never loaded. Every Google avatar on the site was blank. Google's image host refuses a request that carries a Referer, and the browser then blocks the response outright; one attribute fixed all of them.

Several times the measurements passed while the screenshot showed a problem: a tonearm clipped off its deck, a provider logo frozen while the text around it moved, sand on a phone that was all there and all moving and still read as noise. All three got caught by looking.

The maker

A solo project, taken end to end

Product thinking, interaction design, visual design, 3D, frontend, backend, database, auth, deployment. 150+ commits over two months, still going.

  • Design. The shelf metaphor, both layouts, the homepage, the dashboard, the settings, the admin.
  • 3D and motion. The turntables and the sand are three.js, modelled and lit by hand; the deck and arc are hand-written geometry, no animation library.
  • Frontend. Next.js 16 App Router, React 19, Tailwind 4, and CSS modules for the homepage.
  • Backend. Prisma 7 on Postgres, ten models, Auth.js v5 with Google and email sign-in, rate limiting per account and per IP, tokens encrypted with AES-256-GCM.
  • Care. Thirteen check suites, ISR-cached public pages, reduced motion honoured throughout, and spindlshare.vercel.app kept as a complete working copy in case the domain ever lapses.
