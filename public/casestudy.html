The problem

Sharing a playlist means sharing an app, not a taste

People's listening is split across services. The playlist you made for a road trip is on Spotify; the one you actually fall asleep to is on YouTube. Sharing all of it means pasting three links and explaining which is which.

A link-in-bio tool can hold those links, but it flattens them into identical rows. A playlist is not a row. It has a cover, a mood, a length, and none of that survives being listed as blue text.

So the brief became narrow on purpose: one page, one link, and the playlists have to look like playlists.

The insight

The cover art is the interface

Every playlist already ships with the only visual anybody recognises. Lead with the artwork and the page explains itself before a word is read.

That reframed the page from a list into a shelf, something you flick through rather than scan. It also set the constraint the rest of the build answered to: if the artwork is the interface, the chrome has to get out of its way.

The front door

Four screens from a stranger to a shelf

The homepage has one job: say what this is, and offer the one button that matters. No feature grid, no pricing table, no scroll-to-learn-more.

Why one button. Every field on a sign-up form is a place to leave. The account exists only to own a username and a list, so there is nothing worth asking for that Google does not already answer.

The email path is built and switched off. It needs a Resend key, which this deployment has no budget for, so the live site offers Google alone. The screen above is real, and the code behind it ships; it turns on with one environment variable.

The craft

Small decisions that make a shelf feel like an object

Two layouts, one shelf

A stacked deck for people with a handful of playlists, an arc carousel for people with many. Same data, same chrome; the owner picks in settings and the public page swaps the component.

Geometry as ratios, not pixels

The deck is a 184px card stepping 40px on both axes in a 393px frame, stored as fractions of the measured stage, so it holds its proportions on any screen instead of needing a constant per breakpoint.

One gesture, one card

Any scroll past a small threshold plays a fixed animation to completion and ignores further input until it lands. A flick and a shove move the deck by exactly the same amount.

Endless without duplication

Position wraps modulo the playlist count, so the shelf runs forever in both directions without the list ever being copied. Both ends fade, so cards dissolve in and out instead of popping at the window edge.

Not a rebuild: this is the shipped Deck component, with the same playlists the live page serves. A card steps 40/184ths of itself on both axes, and position wraps modulo the count so the run never ends.

Arc, running live

Same input, a different shelf.

The shipped Arc component. Covers ride a circle of radius 2.1 times the stage height, and a mask dissolves the ends before they can reach the chrome.

The arc's ends don't get cut off. They blur as they go, and a mask on the stage dissolves them before they can touch the header.

Culling covers at the band's edge ended the ribbon on a hard line. Fading each one individually let a cover sit at 0.39 opacity with half of it over the header. The mask is what actually guarantees nothing paints there.

The constraint

The API I designed for wasn't available to me

The original plan was to connect Spotify and YouTube, import every playlist, and store the songs. I built that: OAuth, token refresh, encrypted at rest, sync reconciliation, the lot.

Then the wall: both services gate the scopes bulk import needs behind an approved-organisation review. A solo project doesn't clear it. Everything I'd built assumed a door that wouldn't open.

So the product pivoted to the one route that works without permission: paste a link. The public oEmbed endpoint returns a playlist's title and cover art to anyone who asks. That's ten fields, and not one of them is a track, which quietly settled what the product could and couldn't promise.

What it cost. The song list I'd designed under each player can't be populated from a pasted link. Rather than leave a promise the data can't keep, the refresh control says what it actually does: re-reads each playlist's name and cover art.

What it saved. No OAuth, no tokens, no approval queue. Adding a playlist went from a four-screen connect flow to pasting a URL, which is how people share playlists anyway.

The process

Built by measuring, not by eyeballing

Motion is the hard part of a shelf, and screenshots lie about it. So every interaction got checked by driving a headless browser and reading the DOM back for real numbers, on a real device size.

  • The spinning icon. Having the class isn't proof. The check reads the running animation and samples its angle twice: 100 degrees, then minus 167.
  • The floating card. The requirement was that nothing moves. Verified by measuring the first playlist's position and the page height before, during and after: 294px and 1021px, unchanged.
  • The label's contrast. A first pass said 20:1 and was wrong, because it was sampling the white text, not the cover behind it. Corrected, the real figure was 1.59:1: illegible. That's what put the gradient behind the titles.

Several times the measurements passed while the screenshot showed a problem: a tonearm clipped off its deck, a provider logo frozen while the text around it moved. Both got caught by looking.

The maker

A solo project, taken end to end

Product thinking, interaction design, visual design, frontend, backend, database, auth, deployment. 132 commits over two months, still going.

  • Design. The shelf metaphor, both layouts, the dashboard, the settings, the admin.
  • Frontend. Next.js 16 App Router, React 19, Tailwind 4; the deck and arc are hand-written geometry, no animation library.
  • Backend. Prisma 7 on Postgres, ten models, Auth.js v5 with Google and email sign-in, rate limiting per account and per IP, tokens encrypted with AES-256-GCM.
  • Care. Twelve check suites, ISR-cached public pages, reduced-motion honoured throughout.
